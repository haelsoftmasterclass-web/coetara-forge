The most expensive mistake a founder can make is to build something nobody needed badly enough to use. It is also one of the most avoidable. Validating a problem before building a product takes days, not months, and it changes what gets built.

## Start with a sharp problem statement

Write the problem in one sentence, from the customer's point of view:

> "[Specific customer] struggles to [specific job] because [specific obstacle], which costs them [time, money or risk]."

If you cannot fill every bracket, you do not yet know enough to build.

## Talk to the people who have the problem

Customer discovery is not a survey and it is not a pitch. It is a series of conversations about what people already do.

- **Ask about the past, not the future.** "Tell me about the last time this happened" is reliable. "Would you use an app that…" is not.
- **Look for workarounds.** Spreadsheets, WhatsApp groups, paid middlemen and manual processes are strong evidence that a problem is real.
- **Follow the money and time.** What are people already spending to deal with this?
- **Listen for intensity.** A problem people mention unprompted matters more than one they agree with when asked.

Aim for conversations with people from the same specific segment until you start hearing the same things repeatedly.

## Separate signals from politeness

People are kind. They will tell you your idea is good. Treat these as weak signals:

- compliments,
- "I would definitely use that",
- interest in a future product.

Treat these as strong signals:

- a clear description of a recent, painful episode,
- an existing workaround they pay for or spend time on,
- a commitment: time for a follow-up, an introduction, a pilot, a letter of intent or a pre-payment.

## Size it honestly

A real problem also needs enough customers who have it and the ability to pay for a solution. Estimate how many customers share the problem, how they buy today and what a solution would be worth to them. Rough numbers are fine. Invented numbers are not.

## Decide what the evidence says

At the end of validation you should be able to answer three questions:

1. Is the problem real for a specific group of customers?
2. Is it painful enough that they will change behaviour to solve it?
3. Is there a credible way to reach and charge them?

If the answers are yes, you have earned the right to build. If not, change the customer, the problem or the approach, and validate again.
